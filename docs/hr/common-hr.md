# More HR questions

These come after “tell me about yourself”. Keep answers short and honest.

---

## Q1. Why this company?

**Answer:**

Name something real: product, users, stack, or how they work. Do not say “I need a job”.

The interviewer wants to know if you chose them, or if you are sending the same answer to every company. Spend ten minutes on their website and job post before the interview. One real detail ("you build a booking app for clinics") sounds far better than a general "you are a great company".

> I like that you build [product] for [users]. The stack is what I already use — .NET and Angular — so I can help from week one. I also like that you talk about [quality / customers / team size] in the job post. That matches how I like to work.

**Cross-question:** What do you know about us?

**Cross-answer:**

Say 2–3 facts you actually checked: product, customers, recent news. If you did not research, say so honestly and recover: “I focused on the role and stack. I would like to hear how you see the product in the next year.”

Making up facts is risky, because they may ask a follow-up and you will be caught. An honest answer plus a good question turns a weak moment into a normal conversation.

---

## Q2. Why are you leaving your current job?

**Answer:**

Talk about what you want next, not what you hate.

If you complain about your boss or company, the interviewer starts to wonder if you will be hard to work with. Talking about growth shows you are moving toward something good, not just escaping something bad.

> I have learned a lot there. I want a bigger slice of backend design and a team that ships this stack at a larger scale. It is a pull toward this role, not a push away from people.

**Cross-question:** If we called your manager, what would they say?

**Cross-answer:**

Give a fair picture: mostly good, with one small honest weak point and what you are doing about it.

> That I deliver, I ask when something is unclear, and I care about not breaking production. They might also say I can be slow to say “this is good enough” — I am working on that.

## Q3. What is your notice period / when can you start?

**Answer:**

Be exact. Do not promise a date you cannot keep.

The company plans hiring around your joining date. If you promise an early date and then cannot make it, you lose trust before your first day. Check your notice period in your contract before the interview so you can answer clearly.

> My notice is [X]. I would start around [date], after a proper handover. If you need someone tomorrow, I should be honest: I cannot walk out on my current team.

A "handover" means passing your work, notes and open tasks to someone else so your current team is not left stuck. Interviewers usually like hearing this, because it shows you will treat them the same way one day.

**Cross-question:** Can you join sooner?

**Cross-answer:**

> I can ask. But I will not promise an earlier date if it hurts my current handover. I would rather start a week later and arrive clean.

## Q4. What are your salary expectations?

**Answer:**

Give a range based on market and the role, or ask theirs first.

Look up what people with similar experience earn for this kind of role in your city before the interview. A range gives both sides room to talk. A single number can either be too low (you lose money) or too high (they stop talking). Asking their range is also fair, because they already have a budget.

> I am looking at [range] for this level in this city, depending on the full package. I am more interested in the work and the team. I am open if the role and growth are right. What range have you set for this position?

"Full package" means everything, not just the monthly salary: bonus, insurance, leave, learning budget and similar benefits.

**Cross-question:** Would you take less to join us?

**Cross-answer:**

> If the role, learning, and total package are strong, we can talk. I do not want to start unhappy about pay. That helps nobody.

Never invent a fake current salary.

Companies often check salary slips or past records. If the number does not match, the offer can be taken back, and the trust is gone.

## Q5. Are you interviewing elsewhere?

**Answer:**

Honest and calm.

Most people interview at more than one place, and the interviewer knows that. A calm answer shows you are in demand but still serious about this role. Do not use other offers as a threat.

> I am speaking with a few teams at the same level, all on a similar stack. I am not in a rush to grab the first offer. I want a good match. This role is high on my list because [reason].

**Cross-question:** If we offer tomorrow, will you accept?

**Cross-answer:**

> I would be excited. I would still want a day to read the offer and ask any last questions. I will not leave you waiting for weeks.

Asking for a short time to read the offer is normal and shows care. Promising a quick reply shows respect for their time too.

## Q6. How do you work in a team?

**Answer:**

> I like small, clear tickets and talking early if something is blocked. I write what I changed in the pull request so review is easy. I am fine pairing on a hard bug. I do not like throwing work over the wall with no context.

In plain words: a "ticket" is one task on the team board. A "pull request" is a request to add your code changes to the main code, where others review it first. "Pairing" means two people working on the same problem together at one screen. "Throwing work over the wall" means handing work to someone else with no explanation and leaving them to figure it out. The interviewer is checking that you make life easier for the people around you.

**Cross-question:** What if a teammate is slow or messy?

**Cross-answer:**

> First I help with a review or a pair session. If it keeps happening, I talk to them privately, and then to the lead if needed. I do not gossip in the group chat.

The order matters: help first, then a private talk, then the lead. Jumping straight to the manager or complaining in public damages trust in the team.

---

## Q7. How do you take feedback?

**Answer:**

> I like it specific: what, why, and an example. I do not love vague feedback like “this feels wrong”. If I disagree, I say why, then I follow the team decision. After the review I actually change the code, not only nod.

Everyone says "I love feedback". What makes this answer believable is the detail: you ask for clear feedback, you speak up if you disagree, and then you act on the decision. That shows you are open but not a pushover.

**Cross-question:** Tell me about hard feedback you got.

**Cross-answer:**

> A lead said my PRs were too big to review. He was right. Now I split API and UI, or at least the risky part first. Reviews got faster and I caught my own mistakes sooner.

A big PR (pull request) is like asking someone to proofread a whole book in one go. They get tired and miss things. Smaller pieces are quicker to check and problems show up earlier.

---

## Q8. How do you prioritize?

**Answer:**

> User-facing broken things first, then security, then the feature we promised this sprint, then polish. If two things are “P1”, I ask the product owner. I do not silently pick my favourite task.

"P1" means priority one, the most urgent level. A "sprint" is a short fixed work period, often two weeks. A "product owner" is the person who decides what the team should build first. The point is that you have a clear order, and when two things are equally urgent you ask the person who owns that decision instead of guessing.

**Cross-question:** What if product and tech disagree?

**Cross-answer:**

> I explain the tech risk in user words: “this can lose data” or “this will be slow on Monday morning”. Then we decide together. If I still think it is dangerous, I escalate once — in writing, and short.

"Escalate" means raising the issue to someone more senior. Doing it once, in writing, keeps a clear record without turning it into a long fight.

## Q9. Tell me about a time you failed.

**Answer:**

Pick a real mistake, own it, and say what you changed after. The fix and the lesson matter more than the mistake.

> I shipped a filter that worked on my data and broke on empty dates in production. I owned it, rolled forward a fix, and added a test for empty dates. I also started using a small “empty and ugly data” checklist before release.

"Rolled forward a fix" means releasing a new corrected version, instead of going back to the old one. "Empty and ugly data" means the messy cases real users create: blank fields, odd values, very long text. Test data on your own machine is often too clean, which is why bugs like this slip through.

**Cross-question:** Who was at fault?

**Cross-answer:**

> I was. QA can miss things, but I owned the code. Blame does not fix the next release.

QA is the testing team. Taking ownership instead of pointing at them shows maturity, and it is exactly what the interviewer wants to hear.

## Q10. How do you keep learning?

**Answer:**

> I learn from the task in front of me first — a slow query, a new Angular API. I read the official docs, not ten random tweets. I keep a small notes file of things that surprised me. I do not chase every new framework.

Tech changes fast, so they want to know you keep up. But learning everything at once is not useful. Learning what the current work needs, from good sources, is like a cook learning the dishes on tonight's menu first instead of buying every new gadget.

**Cross-question:** Last thing you learned?

**Cross-answer:**

Pick something true from last month, for example: “signals in Angular”, “EF ExecuteUpdate”, “why not to block on `.Result`”. One sentence of what you used it for.

Keep it real and recent. They may ask a follow-up, so pick something you can explain in simple words.

---

## Q11. Are you a team player or do you like to work alone?

**Answer:**

> Both. I like quiet time to write the hard part. I like the team for design, review, and “is this even the right feature?”. I do not hide in a corner for two weeks.

Picking only one side sounds extreme. Most real work needs both: focus time to build, and team time to check you are building the right thing in the right way.

**Cross-question:** Remote or office?

**Cross-answer:**

Say your real preference and that you can work with theirs. Do not pretend you love an open office if you do not.

If you pretend now, you may end up in a setup that makes you unhappy. Being honest but flexible is the safest answer.

---

## Q12. What do you do if you do not know the answer?

**Answer:**

> I say I do not know yet. I say how I would find out: docs, debugger, a small test, ask the person who last touched it. I do not guess in production. In an interview I can think out loud.

Nobody knows everything. The interviewer wants to see what you do at the edge of your knowledge. A clear plan to find the answer is worth more than a confident guess that turns out wrong. "Think out loud" means saying your reasoning step by step, so they can follow how you approach the problem even if you do not reach the final answer.

**Cross-question:** Is it bad to say “I don’t know”?

**Cross-answer:**

> It is better than a fake answer. Say it, then show how you would learn. That is what seniors do.

## Q13. Describe your ideal manager.

**Answer:**

> Someone who gives clear goals and fast feedback, and protects the team from random urgent work every hour. Someone who lets me own a feature and does more than just talk at standup. I do not need a friend. I need someone I can trust.

This question also tells them what kind of support you need to do well. Avoid describing your current manager's opposite, as it can sound like a complaint. A "standup" is the short daily team meeting where each person says what they are working on.

**Cross-question:** What if your manager is very hands-on?

**Cross-answer:**

> I can work with that at the start. After I have shown I deliver, I would ask for more space, with a plan so they still see progress.

A hands-on manager checks your work closely. This answer shows you can adjust instead of getting annoyed, and that you earn more freedom by delivering.

## Q14. How do you handle on-call / production pressure?

**Answer:**

> First: stop the bleeding. Then find the cause. I write a short timeline: what we saw, what we did, what we will change. I do not keep restarting servers on a guess without taking notes.

"On-call" means being the person who responds if the live system breaks, sometimes outside office hours. "Stop the bleeding" means first getting users working again, even with a quick fix, before hunting for the real cause. It is like a first-aider stopping the bleeding before working out what caused the injury. The timeline helps the team learn from the problem so it does not happen again.

**Cross-question:** Can you work late?

**Cross-answer:**

> For a real outage, yes. As a daily habit, no. Burnout helps nobody. I prefer we fix the root so nights stay quiet.

"Outage" means the system is down or not working for users. The answer shows you are willing in a real emergency, but you also care about fixing problems properly so emergencies do not become normal.

## Q15. Do you have leadership experience?

**Answer:**

Even without the title:

Leadership is not only a job title. Guiding a piece of work, helping others and owning the result all count. Be honest about what you have and have not done.

> I have led a feature: broke it into tasks, helped a junior, talked to QA, and owned the release. I have not managed people on paper. I have been the person others ping when the build is red.

"The build is red" means the automatic check that compiles and tests the code has failed, so the team cannot move forward until someone fixes it.

**Cross-question:** How do you help a junior?

**Cross-answer:**

> I do not take the keyboard and finish it. I sit with them, ask what they already tried, and let them type. I review kindly, but I do not rubber-stamp bad security.

Doing the work for them is faster today but teaches nothing. Letting them type is like teaching someone to drive: they learn by holding the wheel, not by watching you. "Rubber-stamp" means approving something without really checking it.

## Q16. Why full-stack, not only backend or only frontend?

**Answer:**

> I like seeing the whole path. A lot of bugs sit in the contract between UI and API. If I can read both, I ship faster and I do not throw blame over the wall. I still go deep on the side the ticket needs.

Backend is the server side: data, rules and APIs. Frontend is what the user sees and clicks. The "contract" is the agreement on what data passes between them. When the screen expects one field name and the API sends another, things break. Someone who understands both sides can find that kind of problem quickly instead of each side saying "it is not my code".

**Cross-question:** Which side is stronger?

**Cross-answer:**

Be honest. Example: “Backend is a bit stronger — EF, auth, APIs. On Angular I am solid with screens, forms, and HttpClient. I am growing on signals and performance.”

Saying "both are equally strong" rarely sounds true. Naming your stronger side, and what you are still learning on the other, sounds honest and gives them a clear picture.

## Additional Questions
### Q17. Why should we not hire you?
**Answer:** I would answer honestly: “If you need someone who has already led a very large team or has deep experience in a stack I have never used, I may not be the immediate fit. My strength is hands-on .NET/Angular product development and learning quickly.”

This is a test of honesty and calm. Do not panic or say "there is no reason". Name a real limit that matters only for some roles, then turn back to what you do well.
### Q18. Tell me about a disagreement with a teammate.
**Answer:** I explain the technical difference, listen to their reason, compare it against the requirement, and agree on a decision. If it is a security or data-loss issue, I escalate rather than silently accept it.

The key idea is to judge both ideas against what the feature actually needs, not against who is right. That keeps it about the work, not about egos. Security and data loss are the exceptions where you should not just go along, because the damage is hard to undo.
### Q19. What motivates you?
**Answer:** I like solving a real problem and seeing the feature work end to end. I also enjoy learning when the problem forces me to understand something deeper.

"End to end" means the whole way, from the database and API to the screen the user clicks. Interviewers ask this to see if what drives you fits the work they have. A real, specific answer works better than "money" or "challenges" on their own.
